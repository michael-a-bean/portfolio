### Household Resilience to Commodity-Price Shocks

This project began with a graduate proposal on agroforestry and coffee-price risk in Tanzania, then developed into Ethiopian panel and predictive studies of agricultural practices and household consumption. Audits documented inconsistent crop and land-use classifications and a household-identifier mismatch that left one wave's geographic covariates unmatched. Treating forest cover as agroforestry and constructing price exposure from contemporaneous crop choices also weakened interpretation. The rebuild uses documented survey definitions and explicit join checks; a later weather extension still requires a correction to the ordering of exposure and outcome recall periods. Research on resilience measurement, commodity-price transmission, and exposure-based designs led me to focus on household adjustment and reconsider what the earlier analyses could establish about resilience or the effects of agricultural practices. For the next study, I propose returning to coffee and distinguishing dependence on coffee receipts from land-based exposure. The study would examine a consumption or coping response, with causal claims dependent on the research design.

### Household Enterprise and Commodity-Price Declines

My graduate NetLogo model examined how commodity prices, household capabilities, credit, and grants affected enterprise participation and inequality. Review found inconsistent borrowing costs between entry decisions and realized income, discrepancies between planned and executed allocations, and artificial input values that allowed production with zero labor or capital. Wealth and grant financing also lacked consistent accounts. I subsequently revised the NetLogo implementation and completed a tested Python migration. The model still requires empirical validation. Research on rural labor markets, seasonal credit, and local demand suggests that enterprise entry can rise while consumption falls, and that finance can sustain farming rather than encourage enterprise entry. I therefore propose studying whether enterprise cushions a crop-price decline when its customers face the same decline, and how seasonal liquidity affects that outcome. The next step is an economic specification with explicit budgets, feasible choices, and customer demand, assessed against simpler analytical models before adding agent interactions.

### Coffee Prices and Household Enterprise in Tanzania

This study revisits published evidence linking coffee-price declines to household enterprise in Tanzania. Before interpreting enterprise ownership as distress coping or welfare protection, I sought to reconstruct the published association and consider competing explanations in the literature involving household income and local demand. I independently reconstructed analyses from the historical Kagera survey and assessed comparable questions in later national panels. The historical association was recovered, but unavailable author code, uncertain price-data vintage, and unresolved unit and sample rules limit exact replication. In my reconstruction, a sample close to the published benchmark includes records of interview attempts and partial interviews coded as having no enterprise. This coding may conflate missing interviews with observed non-ownership. I therefore report a completed-interview comparison, document unresolved construction choices, and keep the surveys separate. Three national-panel designs stopped before estimation because of inadequate support or precision; these are feasibility findings rather than evidence of no response. The reconstruction and feasibility report is complete. Further empirical claims about enterprise as coping will require additional source materials or a new research design.

### Land Tenure and Land Conditions in Nigeria

This project grew out of the Tanzania research and the question of whether uncertain access to farmland discourages investment with longer-term returns. A documentation assessment across eight LSMS-ISA countries led to Nigeria's 2023–24 survey. Early screening procedures became unnecessarily restrictive, while source audits exposed ambiguities in questionnaire routing and survey design that affected sample construction and uncertainty estimates. Review of African tenure and conservation research showed that effects depend on particular rights, investment horizons, and institutions, and that reported practices do not establish improved land condition. I therefore analyzed land-use arrangements, documentation, reported acquisition dates, and perceived security separately. I simplified screening and kept measures and eligible populations separate in survey-weighted descriptive analyses, with sensitivity checks for missing acquisition years and tenure composition. Tenure dimensions associated with perceived security did not relate uniformly to reported erosion or erosion-control and water-harvesting facilities. I have completed a manuscript outline for a paper focused on measurement. The descriptive results inform the broader question of how land rights affect investment horizons, but do not establish causal effects.

### Artificial Life and Adaptive Networks

These experiments began by asking whether recurrent networks governed by local learning rules could develop persistent, differentiated organization without an externally scored task. I used large language models (LLMs) to generate simulation code and assist with analysis and interpretation. My verification and recordkeeping did not keep pace with those revisions: implementation errors survived repeated model-assisted reviews, while missing conversations and incomplete links between code versions and saved runs limited later reconstruction. An audit found that the update order deleted newly added connections before they could learn, while some apparent functional differences reflected unequal test inputs or spatial geometry. I preserved reproducible results and reconstructed key cases in a small reference package with explicit update rules, matched-input controls, and recorded configurations. Research on structural plasticity, continual learning, and artificial life revealed close precedents for these experiments. It also suggested a more specific question about how networks retain and acquire responses as their connections change. The reference reconstruction is complete. A possible next study would reproduce an established local-learning model, then test how connection maintenance and the allocation of learning rates affect retention and reacquisition after inputs change.

### Sugarscape and Learned Decision Rules

This project began as a Mesa reimplementation of Sugarscape, then added interchangeable decision modules to examine how natural-language prompts affect movement, trade, social actions, and consumption. Archived experiments crossed four prompts with five decision configurations. API cost and latency motivated coarse caching that reused decisions across distinct states, while invalid responses invoked default actions. The current movement code also counts an allowed stay-in-place response as invalid. These features require separating decision quality, software errors, and cache effects before interpreting population outcomes. Drawing on research on generative agent models, behavioral validation, and computational cost, I developed a revised study design with unprompted, irrelevant-prompt, and rule-restatement controls. It also proposes checks of prompt paraphrases and decision reuse. These comparisons have not yet been run. Future work would compare generative LLMs with [TypeSafe's Jev](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev) or similar models that select among predefined actions. Matched states, rules, and simulation settings would support comparisons of behavior, response reliability, latency, and cost. The study would test whether a learned decision module adds useful behavioral variation beyond simpler rules. Prompt sensitivity alone would not establish human-like motives or realistic social behavior.
